Lesson 1The threat: which locks break, and why

Every classical public-key scheme rests on a trapdoor: a calculation that is easy forward and impractical backward. Quantum algorithms make the backward direction practical.

FamilyHard problem it relies onClassicalQuantum
RSAFactor n = p·qsecurebroken
Diffie-Hellman, ElGamal, MQVDiscrete logarithmsecurebroken
Elliptic curve suiteDiscrete log on a curvesecurebroken
Symmetric ciphersNo number-theory trapdoorsecureweakened, not broken
HashesNo number-theory trapdoorsecureweakened, not broken

Where it bites. Nearly every network connection starts with an asymmetric handshake: e-commerce, banking, VPNs, plane tickets, online classes. Cryptocurrencies link blocks with hashes (fine) but prove ownership with public-key signatures (not fine).

Candidate replacements named in the course: CRYSTALS-Kyber (NIST standard), FrodoKEM (reached round 3, not selected, used by the German government), and NTRU (one of the most-studied lattice schemes).

Lesson 1Modular arithmetic, congruence, the totient

The modulus is the edge of the universe

Programmers say "divide and keep the remainder." Mathematicians say "do all your arithmetic inside a world of size m." The clock is the everyday version: 4 o'clock plus 10 hours is 14, but the clock has no 14, so it lands on 2.

8 mod 5 = 3   13 mod 5 = 3   18 mod 5 = 3   →   8 ≡ 13 ≡ 18 (mod 5)

Relatively prime, and Euler's totient φ(n)

Two numbers are relatively prime (co-prime) if they share no factor other than 1. The totient φ(n) counts how many integers below n are relatively prime to it. Euler worked this out in the 1700s for no practical reason at all; RSA found the reason 200 years later.

Shortcut 1 — primes. Nothing below a prime shares a factor with it, so φ(p) = p − 1. φ(7) = 6, φ(11) = 10, φ(13) = 12.

Shortcut 2 — products of two primes. φ(p·q) = φ(p)·φ(q) = (p−1)(q−1). No enumeration needed, however large p and q are.

"That's all fifth-grade math." Pick primes, multiply, take a totient, solve one equation. The letters are deliberate: e encrypts, d decrypts.

Why factoring kills it. n = 187 is in the public key. Factor it and you have p = 17, q = 11 → m = 160 → with the public e, solving for d takes moments. For a 2048-bit n a classical computer cannot factor; a quantum computer running Shor's algorithm can.

Lesson 2Vectors, independence, dimension

A vector is a list of numbers; drawn, it is an arrow. Length comes from Pythagoras. The question that matters for lattice cryptography is whether a set of vectors is linearly independent: can you combine them with scalars (not all zero) and land back on zero? If yes, they're dependent — one of them is redundant.

Vector space, informally. A self-contained world: add any two vectors in it, or scale one by a real number, and you're still in it. "Closed under addition and scalar multiplication." A line through the origin is one; so is a plane; so are spaces too big to draw — which is where lattices live.

A vector space is the same idea with vectors as the elements. That is why sets, vector spaces, and abstract algebra are one lesson: they are all closed worlds you can compute inside, and the functions between them are the injective/surjective/bijective maps from earlier.

Lesson 2Polynomial rings and the truncated ring

This is the ring NTRU and the lattice KEMs live in. The elements are polynomials; the modulus wraps them into a finite world, just as mod 12 wrapped the clock.

Example, N = 3, q = 7:  (2 + 3x + x²)·(1 + x) = 2 + 5x + 4x² + x³  →  x³ = 1  →  3 + 5x + 4x²  (all coefficients already < 7)

Where lesson 2 is heading. Pick N independent vectors in a big space → they generate a lattice. Finding the shortest vector in that lattice is hard for classical and quantum computers. The truncated ring is the compact way to write those vectors down. That is the bridge from this lesson's vocabulary to NTRU, FrodoKEM and Kyber.

Lesson 4Lattices, and the problems that are hard on them

RSA hides a secret behind factoring. Lattice cryptography hides it behind a geometry question: find the shortest vector, or the closest one, in a grid too big to look at.

A lattice is a grid built from basis vectors

Take a few linearly independent vectors (lesson 2). Every integer combination of them — add, subtract, repeat — is a lattice point. Those starting vectors are the basis. The same lattice has many bases.

Drag the ● target — solving CVP with each basis

Babai's rounding is the obvious algorithm: write the target in the basis, round both coefficients to whole numbers, and take the lattice point that lands on. With a short orthogonal basis it finds the nearest point every time. With a long skewed one, rounding happens along the wrong directions and the answer slides away — the same trapdoor NTRU and GGH are built on. Drag the dot, or focus it and use the arrow keys.

ProblemAskUsed by
Shortest vector (SVP)Given a basis, find the shortest non-zero lattice vector, measured by a norm (a function giving every vector a positive length).NTRU
Gap-SVPDecide only: is the shortest vector ≤ 1, or > β(n)? A yes/no version — most security proofs reduce to this.proofs
Closest vector (CVP)Given a point v not on the lattice, find the lattice point nearest it. SVP is roughly the special case where v is the origin.GGH (1997)
Gap-CVPDecide: is some lattice point within 1 of v, or are all points farther than β?proofs
Short integer solution (SIS)Find a short vector x with A·x = 0 mod q.Ajtai (1996, since broken as a cryptosystem)
Learning with errors (LWE)Recover s from noisy linear equations A·s + e mod q.Kyber, Dilithium, FrodoKEM

Basis reduction is the attack. LLL and BKZ (both built on Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization) take a bad basis and hunt for a shorter, nearly orthogonal one — turning the right-hand picture into the left. They help, the way number-field sieves help against RSA, but not enough: at real sizes the problems stay infeasible, and no known quantum speed-up changes that.

GGH was published in 1997 and ignored for years: correct, but no better than RSA at the time. When quantum resistance became the question, it and several other lattice schemes "had the dust knocked off." A lot of post-quantum cryptography is old mathematics re-examined.

Lesson 4What "secure" means: IND-CPA, IND-CCA, IND-CCA2

A scheme is indistinguishable if an attacker cannot tell a target ciphertext from random noise. The label says what the attacker was allowed to do first.

Why it matters for the LWE scheme above. The step-by-step encryption is only IND-CPA. A CCA2 attacker sends slightly malformed ciphertexts and watches which ones the receiver decodes to 0 or 1, or rejects; each answer leaks a little about the errors, and the errors give up s. ML-KEM closes this with the Fujisaki–Okamoto transform: on decryption, recompute the ciphertext from the recovered message and refuse anything that does not match exactly. The wrapper, not the lattice, is what earns the IND-CCA2 claim.

Lesson 5NTRU: RSA's shape, in a polynomial ring

Hoffstein, Pipher and Silverman, 1996. Reached NIST round 3, not selected, but faster than RSA on the same hardware and one of the most-studied lattice schemes — which makes it the best teaching algorithm in the course.

Roll a fresh key and watch it work again

New random ternary f, g, m and r, with both inverses recomputed from scratch in the same ring. The polynomials change every time; the three decryption steps never do.

Why it works: the p·r·g term is built to vanish

Practical notes from the course. IEEE P1363.1, the lattice public-key standard, recommended NTRU while it was active. Open-source Java implementations exist and a .NET library project was under way; the math is handled for you. Easttom has published NTRU cryptanalysis himself.

Who uses it. Cloudflare (CIRCL library), Amazon hybrid modes, IBM's 2019 quantum-safe tape drive with Kyber and Dilithium; funding from the European Commission, Swiss NSF, Dutch NWO and others. Ask vendors which PKCS/FIPS standards they implement.

"Kyber is broken" headlines. The paper the course cites was a side-channel attack on one implementation — reading physical leakage from a device — not a break of the algorithm. Hard to mount, easy to headline. Distinguish the two whenever you read such a claim.

Lesson 8The other families

Lattices took most of the slots, but four other mathematical families were in the race. Knowing them tells you what the NIST alternates and the hash-based standard actually are.

Isogenies, in one picture

Where the course leaves you. Lattice schemes on LWE are the standards and the bulk of deployment. Code-based (Classic McEliece, HQC) and hash-based (SLH-DSA, LMS, XMSS) are the structurally different backups. Multivariate is mostly history; isogenies are research again. Knowing the map is what lets you read a vendor's data sheet.
